That's a short review, but it names three specific things: competence, work ethic, and communication. Each one is worth unpacking, because each one is something you can actually check for before you hire anyone to work in your home.

## What does "professional and knowledgeable" actually look like on a restoration job?

On a restoration job, professional and knowledgeable means a crew diagnoses the damage before they start tearing anything out, instead of guessing. That includes identifying what's actually wet or burned, how far it has traveled behind walls or under flooring, and what can be dried or cleaned versus what has to come out.

Restoration work in the US generally follows standards published by the [IICRC](https://www.iicrc.org/), the industry body that sets drying, cleaning, and remediation protocols technicians train and certify against. A crew that references those standards, rather than working off a hunch, is doing the job the way it's supposed to be done. That's the difference between someone running a fan in the general direction of a wet carpet and someone setting drying equipment based on a moisture reading and checking it daily until the structure is actually dry.

## Why does "hard working" matter so much in this line of work?

Restoration work is physical, often for long stretches, and it rarely happens on a convenient schedule. Extracting standing water, hauling out ruined drywall and flooring, moving and inventorying contents, and running heavy equipment for days at a time is not a light job.

A crew that's still showing up, still moving fast, and still being careful with your belongings on day three or four of a job is doing something harder than it looks from the outside. That consistency, not just the first impression on day one, is usually what separates a job that goes well from one that drags on.

## Why does answering questions matter as much as the work itself?

Answering questions matters because a restoration job is also an insurance claim, a budget conversation, and a disruption to someone's daily life, all at once. A crew that explains what they're doing and why, in plain language, takes some of that pressure off the homeowner.

That's also why documentation matters. Photos of the damage, a written scope of what's being removed and why, and a clear explanation of what the drying or cleaning process will involve all protect the homeowner if a question comes up later with the insurance adjuster. If you're trying to figure out what any of this should look like before you hire someone, [our guide to choosing a restoration company](/blog/choosing-a-restoration-company/) walks through the specific questions worth asking.

## What should you look for when you're comparing restoration companies?

Look for a company that can show you certifications, licensing, and a clear process, not just a quote. Ask who will actually be on site, whether they're IICRC trained, and how they'll keep you updated while the work is underway.
