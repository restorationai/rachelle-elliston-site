A licensed contractor (NV Class B or B6) manages subcontractors, pulls permits, and coordinates the full scope so you get a finished, code-compliant result without juggling a dozen separate trades. If you're staring at a gutted interior after a water loss, a fire, or a mold remediation and wondering who puts it all back together, you're in the right place. Reconstruction is not the same as mitigation. Mitigation stops the damage. Reconstruction is the build phase that follows. In Boulder City, where older neighborhoods near Lake Mead and the historic downtown district include homes built in the 1940s and 1950s, that rebuild often surfaces surprises: outdated wiring, galvanized pipe, asbestos-era materials, and framing that doesn't meet current Clark County code. A general contractor who understands both the restoration side and the construction side can manage that complexity without sending you back to square one.

## What Does a General Contractor Actually Do on a Reconstruction Project?

A general contractor is the single point of accountability for your entire rebuild. They hire, schedule, and supervise every subcontractor (drywall, electrical, plumbing, flooring, painting), pull the required permits from Clark County Building Department, coordinate inspections, and hand you a finished space that passes final sign-off. You make one call instead of eight.

On a post-disaster reconstruction, the scope typically includes:

- **Structural framing repair**: replacing water-damaged or fire-weakened studs, joists, and sheathing
- **Drywall installation and finishing**: hanging, taping, texturing to match the existing wall profile
- **Flooring**: subfloor repair plus finish flooring (tile, LVP, hardwood, carpet)
- **Painting**: primer coat over new drywall, finish coat matched to existing rooms
- **Trim and millwork**: baseboards, door casings, window trim
- **Cabinetry and fixtures**: kitchen and bath rebuilds after a major loss often require full cabinet replacement
- **Mechanical coordination**: scheduling licensed electricians and plumbers for any affected systems

On a planned renovation (not disaster-related), the scope is similar but starts from design intent rather than a damage assessment. Either way, the permit and inspection process is the same.

## How Does the Reconstruction Process Work, Step by Step?

Reconstruction follows a defined sequence. Skipping steps creates problems at inspection and can void your insurance coverage for the rebuild.

1. **Scope of work and estimate**: The contractor walks the site after mitigation is complete (or after design drawings are finalized for a renovation), documents every affected system and surface, and produces a written scope with line-item pricing. This document is also what your insurance adjuster uses to approve the rebuild claim.
2. **Permit application**: Clark County requires permits for structural, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work. The contractor files the application, pays the fee, and posts the permit on-site before work begins. Unpermitted work is a liability that follows the property title.
3. **Demolition and prep**: Any remaining damaged material is removed. Framing is inspected for moisture content before new drywall goes up. The IICRC S520 standard (for mold) and S500 standard (for water damage) both require documented dry standards before enclosure.
4. **Rough-in inspections**: Electrical, plumbing, and framing inspections happen before walls are closed. The inspector signs off on the rough-in before drywall installation begins.
5. **Finish work**: Drywall, flooring, paint, trim, cabinets, and fixtures are installed in sequence.
6. **Final inspection**: Clark County issues a final inspection sign-off. This is the document that closes the permit and protects you if you sell the property.
7. **Punch list**: Any items the inspector flags or that don't meet the agreed scope are corrected before the contractor is paid in full.

For insurance-covered rebuilds, the contractor typically works directly with the adjuster on supplement requests when hidden damage is discovered mid-project. This is normal and expected, especially in older Boulder City homes.

## What Should You Look for in a Reconstruction Contractor in Boulder City?

Not every contractor is equipped to handle post-disaster reconstruction, and not every restoration company carries a general contracting license. Here's what to verify before signing anything.

**Nevada contractor license**: Nevada requires a Class B general building contractor license (or a B6 specialty license for specific trades) for projects over $1,000. Verify the license at the Nevada State Contractors Board (contractors.nv.gov). An unlicensed contractor cannot legally pull permits, which means inspections don't happen and your insurance company may deny the rebuild claim.

**Insurance and bonding**: The contractor must carry general liability and workers' compensation. Ask for certificates of insurance naming you as an additional insured. If a subcontractor is injured on your property and the GC isn't properly insured, you can be held liable.

**Experience with insurance-covered rebuilds**: Post-disaster reconstruction involves Xactimate scoping, supplement negotiations, and documentation that most residential remodelers have never done. Ask specifically whether the contractor has worked with insurance adjusters and can provide a scope of work in the format adjusters require.

**Written scope before work begins**: Never agree to time-and-materials billing on a reconstruction without a not-to-exceed cap. A fixed-price or detailed line-item scope protects you from open-ended costs.

**References from similar projects**: A kitchen remodel reference doesn't tell you much about a fire rebuild. Ask for references from projects that involved insurance claims or post-disaster work specifically.

## What Happens If You Skip the Permit?

Unpermitted work creates three problems. First, the work can't be inspected, so defects in electrical, plumbing, or structural systems go undetected until they cause a loss. Second, when you sell the property, unpermitted work must be disclosed and often must be demolished and rebuilt with permits before closing. Third, your insurance company can deny a future claim if the unpermitted work contributed to the loss. In Boulder City, Clark County enforces permit requirements and can issue stop-work orders and fines for unpermitted construction. The permit fee is a small cost compared to the exposure.
