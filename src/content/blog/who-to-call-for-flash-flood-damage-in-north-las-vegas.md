## What Should You Do in the First 15 Minutes?

Cut power to any affected area before you step into standing water, and only if you can reach the breaker panel without walking through it. Flash flood water and electrical panels are a dangerous combination, and if the panel itself is wet or the water is more than ankle-deep near outlets, stay out and call your utility provider instead of guessing.

Once the area is safe, take photos and a short video of the water line, the affected rooms, and anything damaged, before you move or discard anything. This is the documentation your insurance adjuster will ask for first. Then move valuables and furniture legs out of standing water if you can do it safely. Don't run a shop vac rated for dry debris into standing water, and don't use a household wet/dry vac as your primary extraction tool for anything more than a few inches. It won't pull water out of carpet pad or wall cavities, and the clock on mold growth starts within 24 to 48 hours either way.

## Who Do You Call First: a Restoration Company, a Plumber, or the Utility?

Call a restoration company first for water that came from outside, a plumber for water still actively flowing from a pipe or fixture, and 911 or NV Energy for anything involving exposed wiring, a submerged panel, or gas smell. Flash flood water from street runoff or an overflowing wash isn't a plumbing problem: there's no leak to shut off, and a plumber doesn't carry extraction equipment or dehumidifiers. That's restoration work.

If the water is still rising or actively entering the home, your city's non-emergency line or the Clark County Regional Flood Control District can tell you whether a wash or drainage channel nearby is still flowing. Once the inflow has stopped and it's safe, the job becomes extraction, drying, and sanitizing, which is what a restoration crew does. Call your insurance company after mitigation starts, not before. Most carriers expect you to limit further damage immediately, and "I waited for the adjuster" is one of the more common reasons a claim gets reduced.

## Is Flash Flood Water Dangerous to Clean Up Yourself?

Yes. Water that enters a home from outside ground-level flooding is classified as Category 3, or black water, under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), meaning it's assumed to carry sewage, chemicals, pesticides, or other contaminants picked up from streets and storm drains. That's different from a clean supply-line leak, and it changes the cleanup entirely.

Category 3 water means porous materials that got wet, carpet pad, baseboards, drywall up to the waterline, and often the lower few inches of insulation, generally need to be removed and discarded rather than just dried. Surfaces that stay need to be cleaned and sanitized, not just dried with a box fan. Drying alone leaves contamination behind and sets up conditions for mold within days, especially in a slab home where moisture has nowhere to go but up into the walls. If sewage backed up as part of the flooding, that's its own category of work; see our [sewage cleanup](/services/sewage-cleanup/) page for what that process involves.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Flash Flood Damage?

Usually not, if the water came from outside ground-level flooding. Standard homeowners policies typically exclude surface flooding and name it specifically as an exclusion, even though they do cover sudden, accidental water damage from inside the home, like a burst pipe. Flood coverage for rising water, storm surge, or overflow from a wash or drainage channel generally requires a separate policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance) or a private flood carrier.

That gap catches a lot of North Las Vegas homeowners off guard, especially those in slab homes near washes where water has entered from the exterior before. The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance) has a plain breakdown of what's typically covered versus excluded. If you're not sure which policy applies to your situation, documenting the source of the water (street runoff vs. a plumbing failure) matters more than anything else in that conversation, and it's one of the first questions an adjuster will ask.
