## How much does smoke damage cleanup cost in North Las Vegas?

Most North Las Vegas smoke cleanup jobs fall between $400 and $9,000, with the spread driven almost entirely by how far the smoke traveled and what kind of smoke it was. A contained kitchen fire that stayed in one room costs a fraction of what whole-house smoke infiltration costs after it's worked through drywall, carpet, and HVAC ductwork.

Protein smoke from a grease or kitchen fire is harder and more expensive to remove than dry smoke from a wood or paper fire, because protein residue is nearly invisible but leaves an intense, sticky odor that bonds to surfaces. Wildfire smoke drift, which North Las Vegas sees during Southern Nevada's summer fire season, usually falls into the lighter, odor-focused tier since it rarely deposits heavy soot indoors.

## What factors change the price of smoke cleanup?

The type of smoke, the number of affected rooms, and how long the residue sat before cleanup started are the three biggest cost drivers. Each one changes both the labor involved and the equipment needed.

Protein smoke (grease, cooking fires) requires degreasing agents and multiple cleaning passes on hard surfaces, which adds labor hours even when the visible damage looks minor. Dry smoke from wood or paper leaves a powdery residue that's faster to wipe down but travels further through a house on air currents, often reaching rooms far from the fire's origin.

HVAC contamination adds real cost. If the system ran during or after the fire, soot and odor particles get pulled into the ductwork and recirculate through every vent in the house. Cleaning or sealing ducts, replacing filters, and sometimes running duct-specific deodorization adds $500 to $1,500 depending on system size.

Time matters too. Soot is acidic, and the longer it sits on surfaces, especially metal fixtures, electronics, and unsealed wood, the more it etches and discolors them. A kitchen fire cleaned within 48 hours costs noticeably less than the same fire addressed a week later.

## Does homeowners insurance pay for smoke cleanup?

Most standard homeowners policies cover smoke damage cleanup as part of fire-related perils, including smoke that originates from a neighboring property. Coverage typically includes professional cleaning, odor removal, and contents restoration, subject to your deductible.

The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) notes that fire and smoke are among the perils covered by a standard HO-3 policy, which is the most common homeowners policy type. That said, coverage specifics (sub-limits on contents, whether smoke odor alone without visible damage qualifies) vary by carrier and policy language, so check your declarations page or call your agent before assuming a figure.

Documentation drives claim outcomes. Photos of affected rooms before cleanup starts, an itemized list of damaged contents, and a written scope of work from whoever does the cleaning all strengthen a claim. A restoration company that documents as it works, rather than after the fact, makes the adjuster's job easier and your payout more likely to match the actual damage.

## What should you throw away after smoke damage?

Porous items that absorbed smoke odor deep into their fibers, rather than just surface soot, usually can't be fully deodorized and are better replaced than cleaned. That includes most soft, absorbent materials that sat close to the fire or in its direct smoke path.

Common items to discard:

- Upholstered furniture and mattresses with heavy smoke exposure
- Carpet padding (even if the carpet itself can be cleaned)
- Unopened food and anything stored without an airtight seal
- Cosmetics, medications, and other items affected by heat or smoke particles
- Plastic items that melted or warped, even slightly

Items worth attempting to save with professional cleaning: clothing and linens (often salvageable with ozone or specialty wet cleaning), hard-surface furniture, most electronics if cleaned promptly, and photos or documents, which specialty content cleaners can often restore even after heavy smoke exposure. ## What does the smoke and odor removal process actually involve?

Smoke cleanup follows a sequence: assessment and documentation, surface cleaning of hard materials, contents triage, and odor treatment using equipment matched to how deep the smoke penetrated. Skipping steps, especially odor treatment, is the most common reason a house still smells like smoke weeks later.

1. **Assessment.** A technician walks the property, documents affected areas and contents, and identifies the smoke type (protein, dry, or wet smoke from a slow-burning fire).
2. **Source removal.** Loose soot is HEPA-vacuumed before any wet cleaning, since wiping soot without vacuuming first tends to grind it deeper into surfaces.
3. **Surface cleaning.** Walls, ceilings, hard flooring, and fixtures are cleaned with agents matched to the smoke type and surface material.
4. **Odor treatment.** This is where thermal fogging, ozone generators, or hydroxyl generators come in. Thermal fogging uses a heated deodorizing agent that penetrates the same way smoke did. Ozone is effective but requires the space to be vacated during treatment. Hydroxyl generators work more slowly but can run in occupied spaces, which matters if displacement isn't an option.
5. **HVAC and duct service**, when the system was contaminated.
6. **Final verification** that no odor remains, often with a walkthrough once the space has been aired out.

The [IICRC's S700 Standard for Professional Fire and Smoke Damage Restoration](https://iicrc.org/s700/) outlines the industry framework most of these steps follow.
