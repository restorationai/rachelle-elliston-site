## What commercial restoration actually involves

Commercial restoration covers the same underlying physics as residential water and fire damage, extraction, drying, soot removal, odor control, but the constraints are different. A loss in an office suite, retail storefront, warehouse, or multi-tenant building has to account for lease terms, tenant improvement finishes, server rooms, point-of-sale systems, inventory, and the fact that part of the building often needs to stay open while another part is under containment. Equipment scales accordingly: commercial-grade desiccant dehumidifiers for large open floor plans, negative air machines to isolate work zones from occupied space, and moisture mapping across suspended ceilings, raised flooring, and HVAC plenums where water and smoke travel further than they do in a typical house. Timelines depend on square footage and loss category, but phased work, drying one zone while another stays operational, is standard on commercial jobs in a way it rarely is on residential ones.

## Our process

1. **Triage and life-safety assessment.** Before anything else, we identify what's structurally unsafe, what's electrically live, and what can be isolated so unaffected parts of the building keep functioning.
2. **Extraction or soot and smoke removal.** Standing water gets pulled first; for fire losses, we characterize the soot type, protein residue from a kitchen fire behaves very differently than synthetic soot from burned plastics and electronics, before selecting a cleaning method.
3. **Containment and phased drying.** We section off active work areas with poly barriers and negative air pressure so retail customers, office staff, or warehouse crews can continue using unaffected space while drying equipment runs.
4. **Moisture monitoring and air quality checks.** Daily readings through drywall, subfloor, and HVAC returns confirm drying progress and catch moisture that's migrated into spaces the initial loss didn't visibly touch.
5. **Documentation for property management and insurance.** Photos, moisture logs, and scope notes go into a report built for a commercial adjuster or a property manager who needs to justify the claim to ownership or a tenant.

## What separates a good commercial restoration response from a bad one

The biggest mistake on commercial losses is treating the whole building like one undifferentiated space. A crew that doesn't section off the affected area ends up shutting down more square footage than necessary, which costs the business more in downtime than the restoration itself. Another common miss is skipping moisture checks above the ceiling grid or under raised flooring, both are common in commercial buildings and both hide moisture that resurfaces as mold weeks later. On fire losses, operators who don't correctly identify soot type often use the wrong cleaning agent, which can smear protein residue instead of lifting it or fail to neutralize odor from synthetic materials. Adjusters reviewing commercial claims look closely at whether drying equipment placement and runtime match the documented moisture readings, and whether the contractor separated the claim into categories, structure, contents, business interruption, that align with how the policy is actually written.

## What does commercial restoration cost?

Commercial restoration costs vary more than residential work because they depend on square footage, occupancy type, and whether the business needs partial access maintained during the job. Every loss gets a written scope before work begins, since a one-room office suite and a 10,000 square foot warehouse aren't priced the same way. Commercial property insurance typically covers the restoration and drying portion of a covered water or fire loss, and often business interruption coverage if the policy includes it, though contents and inventory loss may fall under a separate line.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

North Las Vegas summers push commercial HVAC systems hard, and a rooftop unit failure or condensate line backup during peak heat can soak ceiling tile and insulation in a commercial space within hours. Flat and low-slope commercial roofs common in the valley's industrial and retail buildings are also more vulnerable to ponding during the region's short, intense monsoon storms, which shows up as water intrusion around rooftop units and parapet walls rather than obvious roof damage from the ground.
