If a room smells musty after you close the door for a few hours, or you've had a slab leak, roof leak, or AC condensation problem in the last year, the question isn't whether mold *might* be growing somewhere behind the drywall. It's whether you can prove what's actually in the air before you spend money on remediation, repairs, or a real estate transaction that hinges on a clean bill of health. Mold inspection and testing gives you that documentation: a defensible, lab-backed answer instead of a guess.

## What mold inspection and testing actually involves

A proper mold assessment starts with a visual inspection of the property, moisture mapping with a meter or thermal camera to find hidden damp spots behind walls, under cabinets, or around window frames, and a conversation about the home's history (past leaks, flood events, humidity complaints, HVAC issues). From there, testing branches into two categories: air sampling, which captures spore counts from the air in a given room compared against an outdoor baseline, and surface sampling (tape lifts or swabs), which confirms what a specific patch of visible growth actually is. Samples go to an independent, accredited lab, not an in-house lab, which matters for insurance and real estate credibility. Turnaround on most lab results runs a few business days. The whole on-site portion of a standard inspection typically takes one to two hours depending on the size of the property and how many samples are pulled.

## Our process

1. **Intake and history review.** We ask about recent leaks, water intrusion, musty odors, or health complaints tied to specific rooms, since that narrows where we look first.
2. **Visual inspection and moisture mapping.** Moisture meters and thermal imaging locate damp framing, subfloor, or wall cavities that aren't visible at the surface.
3. **Sample collection.** Air samples establish spore counts indoors versus outdoors; surface swabs or tape lifts confirm the identity of visible growth when present.
4. **Independent lab analysis.** Samples go to a third-party accredited laboratory, not graded in-house, so the results hold up for insurance claims or real estate disclosures.
5. **Written report and recommendations.** You get a clear report with lab findings, photos, moisture readings, and, if remediation is warranted, a scope of work our IICRC AMRT-trained team can carry out.

## What separates a good mold inspection from a bad one

The most common shortcut is skipping moisture mapping and going straight to air sampling. Spore counts without knowing the moisture source behind them tell you there's a problem but not where it's coming from or whether it's getting worse. Another mistake is relying on a single air sample in one room and calling it a whole-house assessment; cross-contamination between HVAC zones means a problem in a bathroom can show up in a bedroom's air sample if the system is recirculating. Adjusters and real estate inspectors also look for a documented outdoor baseline sample, since without one, indoor spore counts have nothing to compare against. A thorough inspection pairs visual evidence, moisture data, and lab results together rather than leaning on just one.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

North Las Vegas doesn't deal with the humidity load that coastal cities do, but mold problems here almost always trace back to a specific water event rather than ambient moisture: a slab leak in an older home, a failed water heater, a roof leak after monsoon season storms between July and September, or condensation from an undersized or poorly maintained HVAC system. Evaporative coolers (swamp coolers), still common on older homes in the valley, are another frequent source of hidden moisture if a pad or drain line fails. Because the desert air dries surfaces quickly, visible mold can look deceptively minor while the material behind it stays saturated, which is exactly why moisture mapping matters more here than spore counts alone.
