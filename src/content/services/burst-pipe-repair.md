A burst pipe rarely announces itself politely. Sometimes it's a copper fitting that finally gives out after decades of mineral buildup, sometimes it's a PEX line nicked during a remodel, and sometimes it's a slab leak that shows up as a warm spot on the tile and a water bill that doesn't match usage. Whatever the source, the water doesn't stay where it started. It travels along joists, wicks up through baseboards, and pools in spots you can't see without pulling back flooring or opening a wall cavity.

## What burst pipe cleanup and repair actually involves

This isn't just mopping up a puddle. A proper response combines two trades working together: identifying and repairing the actual pipe failure, and extracting and drying everything the water touched before it turns into a secondary mold problem. That means shutting down the water supply at the source, locating the break (which sometimes requires moisture meters or thermal imaging if it's inside a wall or under a slab), repairing or replacing the failed section of pipe, and then running extraction and drying equipment on the structure itself. Depending on how long the line was leaking before it was found, you could be looking at anything from a damp baseboard to saturated subfloor across multiple rooms.

## Our process

1. **Locate and stop the source.** We confirm where the pipe failed, whether it's a supply line, a water line break under the slab, or a fitting behind a wall, and shut off water to that section.
2. **Extract standing water.** Pumps and wet vacs pull out free water before it spreads further into flooring, baseboards, or adjoining rooms.
3. **Repair the pipe.** Depending on the material (copper, galvanized, or PEX) and location, our licensed crew repairs or replaces the damaged section so the system is safe to pressurize again.
4. **Dry the structure.** Air movers and dehumidifiers go to work on wall cavities, subfloor, and cabinetry, with moisture readings tracked daily against the IICRC S500 drying standard rather than guessed at.
5. **Verify and close up.** We confirm moisture content in framing and flooring is back to a normal range before any drywall, insulation, or flooring goes back in.

## What separates a good pipe burst response from a bad one

The mistake we see most often is treating a burst pipe like a surface cleanup problem instead of a structural drying problem. A crew that mops the visible water and leaves a box fan running isn't addressing what's soaked into the wall cavity, the subfloor, or the insulation batting behind the drywall. Drywall and insulation that look dry on the surface can still be holding enough moisture to support mold growth within 24 to 48 hours.

Adjusters reviewing these claims generally want documentation: moisture readings at the start and throughout drying, photos of the failed pipe section, and a clear description of what was removed versus dried in place. A response that skips that documentation tends to run into disputes later over what was actually necessary. Another common gap is not checking adjacent rooms. Water from a burst pipe in a wall often travels sideways along framing before it shows up somewhere unexpected, like a closet two rooms over.
