## What Trauma Scene Cleanup actually involves

Trauma scene cleanup covers the decontamination and restoration of spaces affected by unattended deaths, violent crime, suicide, or serious accidents. The work goes beyond visible cleanup: technicians assess porous and non-porous materials separately, since carpet, drywall, and subflooring absorb fluids in ways that tile or sealed concrete do not. Affected materials that can't be fully decontaminated are removed and disposed of as regulated medical waste. Crews work in full PPE, use hospital-grade disinfectants and enzymatic cleaners, and follow OSHA bloodborne pathogen protocols throughout. Depending on the scope, a single-room job may take a few hours; scenes involving subfloor saturation or multiple rooms can run a full day or more.

## Our process

1. **Scene assessment and containment.** We confirm the scene is released by law enforcement or the medical examiner, then isolate the affected area to prevent cross-contamination to the rest of the property.
2. **PPE staging and biohazard removal.** Technicians suit up in protective equipment rated for bloodborne pathogen exposure and remove visible biological material along with any materials that have absorbed it.
3. **Enzymatic and hospital-grade disinfection.** Surfaces are treated with enzymatic cleaners that break down residual biological matter at a molecular level, followed by hospital-grade disinfection of all affected and adjacent surfaces.
4. **Odor neutralization.** Trauma scenes often carry odor that standard air fresheners can't mask. We use deodorization treatments that address the source rather than cover it over.
5. **Regulated waste manifesting and disposal.** All biohazard waste is bagged, labeled, and disposed of through licensed medical waste channels with documentation kept for the property owner's records.

## What separates a good trauma scene cleanup response from a bad one

The biggest technical mistake in this field is treating the job as a surface cleaning task instead of a decontamination task. Fluids travel along subfloor seams, down wall cavities, and into grout well past where they're visible, and a crew that only wipes down what it can see leaves biological material behind. A thorough response tests and treats beyond the visible stain, documents what was removed versus treated in place, and never guesses on porous materials, if there's doubt, the material comes out. Insurance adjusters reviewing these claims look for clear waste manifests, before-and-after documentation, and a defined scope that matches what was billed. Sloppy documentation is one of the most common reasons claims get delayed.
