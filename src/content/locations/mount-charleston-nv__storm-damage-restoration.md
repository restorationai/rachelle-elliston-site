## Why Mount Charleston Properties See Storm Damage Issues

Mount Charleston sits in the Spring Mountains well above the valley, where dense stands of ponderosa pine and white fir surround most cabins and homes. That tree cover is part of what makes the area livable in summer, but it's also the biggest storm liability: heavy snow load, high wind gusts funneling through Kyle Canyon and Lee Canyon, and monsoon downpours all increase the odds of a limb or whole tree coming down on a roof, deck, or exterior wall. Many structures up here are older wood-frame cabins built for a mountain climate, with steep rooflines, exposed rafter tails, and wood siding that takes on water fast once a roof membrane is breached. Add in the narrow, winding access roads and steep grades, and a tree-damage call here often means careful rigging and shoring before any water extraction or tarping can even start. Flash flooding is the other pattern worth naming: a fast monsoon cell over the canyon can send runoff and debris downhill into lower-lying cabins and crawlspaces within minutes, even if the home itself never takes a direct hit.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Mount Charleston

The first visit is about stabilizing the structure, not demoing it. We assess roof and wall penetrations from fallen trees or wind-driven debris, check for compromised framing before anyone walks a damaged roofline, and get tarps or board-up in place to stop ongoing water intrusion. From there we move into water extraction and structural drying following IICRC S500 guidelines, which matters more in a mountain cabin than a valley home because older wood framing and tongue-and-groove paneling hold moisture longer than modern drywall assemblies. Our IICRC-certified technicians document everything with moisture readings and photos as we go, since insurers want a clear record of what the storm did versus pre-existing wear on an older structure. Once the structure is dry and secure, we scope the repair, which on Mount Charleston properties frequently includes roof decking, siding, and sometimes a damaged deck or retaining wall where runoff undercut the soil.

## Local note

One thing that catches a lot of homeowners off guard: a tree that falls across a Mount Charleston roofline often causes more hidden damage underneath the visible hole than the hole itself suggests, because the impact can crack rafters or shift roof sheathing several feet away from the point of contact.
